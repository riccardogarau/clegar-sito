An SPI that returns to 1: the delay earned value stops seeing

Week twelve of a campaign planned for ten. The contractor's progress report shows an SPI of 0.98, and the contract calls for a recovery plan only when the index falls below 0.90. The number is correctly computed, and the campaign will finish three weeks late. The threshold stopped triggering two weeks ago, in exactly the period when the delay is at its largest.

This article shows why the SPI of a late project returns towards 1 on its own, how the same index is computed in time instead of money, which objections come up when it is proposed, and what to write into the specification of progress reports so that the delay is read in weeks.

Why the SPI returns to 1

In earned value management the schedule is translated into a planned value (PV) curve: how much work, measured in budget, is expected to be complete at each date. Earned value (EV) is the work actually completed, measured against the same budget. The schedule index is their ratio, SPI = EV / PV, and the variance is their difference, SV = EV − PV.

PV, however, stops growing at the planned finish date, when it reaches the budget at completion (BAC). From then on the SPI equals EV / BAC: it no longer compares the work done with the work planned for that date, but with the total work. It is the percentage complete, and at the end it equals 1 for any project, on time or late. In the same way SV returns to zero. Walt Lipke, who in 2003 proposed the alternative described below, points to this as the known flaw of schedule indicators in money: at project end SV = 0 and SPI = 1, even when the project finished late.

The flaw is not confined to the end. Before the planned date, when the PV curve flattens because only closing activities remain, PV grows more slowly and the SPI rises, even if the work proceeds at the same pace as before.

The same index, in time

Earned schedule (ES) asks the opposite question: not how much work is missing against today's date, but on which date the schedule expected to reach the work done so far. On the PV curve one finds the last period C in which PV does not exceed EV, and interpolates linearly within the next period: ES = C + (EV − PVC) / (PVC+1 − PVC).

With the time elapsed, the actual time (AT), the indicators in time follow: SV(t) = ES − AT, in weeks, and SPI(t) = ES / AT. ES never exceeds the planned duration (PD), while AT keeps growing: for a late project SPI(t) stays below 1 up to the last day. The ratio PD / SPI(t) also gives an estimate of the final duration, if the pace stays as measured.

From the third to the eighth week the two indices say the same thing: the SPI lies between 0.73 and 0.77, the SPI(t) between 0.77 and 0.80, and the campaign is producing at about three quarters of the planned pace. Then they part. From the ninth week the PV curve flattens and the SPI rises, while the SPI(t), which measures the pace against the schedule, stays between 0.78 and 0.79. From the tenth, PV is fixed at €2,000k, and the SPI becomes the percentage complete.

In week twelve SV is −€50k, 2.5% of the budget: it looks like a remainder. SV(t) is −2.5 weeks. And the duration estimate obtained with the SPI, PD / SPI, gives 10.3 weeks: a date already almost two weeks in the past. The one obtained with the SPI(t) lies between 12.5 and 13.0 weeks from the third week onwards.

The threshold that stops triggering

The contract in the example calls for a recovery plan when the SPI falls below 0.90. The threshold triggers from the second to the tenth week and stops in the eleventh, when the SPI reaches 0.93: the delay at that point is 2.3 weeks, and at the end it will be 3. The SPI(t) stays below 0.90 from the second week to the last.

A threshold on the SPI is not wrong in itself. It goes blind in the last stretch of the schedule, which is also where a delay costs most: the vessel is still at sea, the following activities are already planned around the contractual date, and the recovery plan has less time to be of use.

The objections

The first: the SPI is the indicator the contract asks for, and it is a standard. Correct, and up to the eighth week of the example it says the same thing as the SPI(t). The problem is not the index, but the use of a ratio between two quantities in money as a measure of a delay in time. Earned schedule is computed from the same PV and EV the contractor already reports: it needs no new data, only one more calculation.

The second: delay is read from the schedule, not from indices. The schedule remains the primary tool, and earned schedule does not replace it. It serves to check it: the finish date the contractor declares when updating the schedule is compared with PD / SPI(t). If the schedule says ten and a half weeks and the index says thirteen, the schedule assumes a recovery of pace that has to be demonstrated. But an ES computed over the whole project measures the volume of work, not the critical path: non-critical work done early can offset a delay on the driving path. Lipke described in 2006 how to apply earned schedule to the critical path activities alone; where the driving path is a single, recognisable one, the index is computed on those activities as well.

The third: the PD / SPI(t) estimate is too simple. It assumes the remaining work proceeds at the pace measured so far, and if the cause of the delay goes away – the weather improves, an instrument is replaced – it overestimates the duration. That is why it is a cross-check, not a date to sign. In the example the pace does not change, and from the third week the estimate stays between 12.5 and 13.0 weeks, against an actual duration of 13.

The fourth: the delay is due to weather, and weather risk sits with the client. That may be true, but it is a different question. The SPI(t) measures how late the work is, not who answers for it: who pays for a day of weather standby is decided by the classification of vessel time, not by a progress index.

The fifth: the baseline has been updated. With a new baseline PV is recomputed, and both the SPI and the SPI(t) restart close to 1. They measure the deviation from the new plan, not from the contractual date. The report must state which baseline they are computed on, and also show the delay against the original one.

What to ask for in the specification

  • 01The delay in weeks, next to the SPI. ES, SV(t) and SPI(t) in every progress report, computed from the same PV and EV, with the interpolation formula written into the specification.
  • 02The threshold on the index in time. The recovery plan is tied to the SPI(t) or to an SV(t) in weeks, not to the SPI, which rises on its own in the last stretch of the schedule.
  • 03The EV measurement rules, decided at kick-off. When a kilometre of line or a deliverable counts as complete: without rules written in advance, every index is an estimate by the contractor.
  • 04The duration estimate as a check. PD / SPI(t) next to the finish date of the updated schedule, with an explanation of the difference when it exceeds one week.
  • 05The index on the driving path. Where a milestone depends on a single path, earned schedule is also computed on the activities of that path.
  • 06The baseline stated. Every index states which baseline it is computed on, and the delay against the original baseline stays in the report even after a revision.

The underlying point

The SPI compares money with money, and at the planned finish date the planned money has run out: from then on the index measures how much work is left, not how much time has been lost. Computed in time, from the same data, the same index stays on the delay up to the last day. Asking for it in the specification costs the contractor nothing, and ties the recovery plan to a number that does not improve on its own.
