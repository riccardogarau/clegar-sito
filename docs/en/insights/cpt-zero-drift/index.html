Zero drift in a CPT: what to read after every test, while the pontoon is still on location

The log of a CPT pushed to 10 m below the seabed shows at the top the reference reading of the cone resistance taken before the test, and at the bottom the one taken after it. They differ by 60 kPa. In dense sand that would be 0.6% of the measured value. In the soft clay of the first few metres it means an undrained shear strength overestimated by half – and the test, under the 2012 edition of ISO 22476-1, still fell within the second class.

This article shows why the same number weighs so differently in the two soils, what the person witnessing the campaign for the client can decide with those two readings while the pontoon is still on location, and what to write into the specification.

Two readings for every test

The cone of a CPT measures forces with load cells, and the output of a load cell at zero load is not stable: it shifts with temperature, with an overload, with soil getting into the filter. That is why ISO 22476-1 provides for a reference reading – the stable output of the sensor just before the cone enters the ground and just after it leaves it – and calls zero drift the absolute difference between the two. The profile is computed from the first reading; the second says by how much that starting point moved during the test.

The two readings say how much, not when. The shift may have happened in the first minutes, while the cone settles to the water temperature, or in the last few metres, because of an overload. In the first case the error affects the whole profile, in the second only the final stretch.

The same shift in two soils

In dense sand cone resistance is measured in MPa: out of 10 MPa, 60 kPa is 0.6%. In soft clay the quantity that matters is a different one. From the cone resistance corrected for pore pressure the total vertical stress of the overlying soil is subtracted, giving the net cone resistance; dividing by an empirically derived cone factor Nkt gives the undrained shear strength su. According to Robertson and Cabal, Nkt typically lies between 10 and 18.

A zero shift in the cone resistance passes in full into the net cone resistance, because neither correction touches it. And in the first few metres of a normally consolidated clay the net cone resistance is around a hundred kPa: the shift is measured against that, not against the MPa of the sand.

A worked example

The figures are synthetic – built to illustrate the mechanism, not taken from real projects.

A cable landfall: CPTU tests from a pontoon in 8 m of water, each pushed to 10 m below the seabed. The first 10 m are soft normally consolidated clay, with a unit weight of 16 kN/m³ and an undrained shear strength that grows from 5 kPa at the seabed by 1.5 kPa per metre. Nkt is 15. The cone has a full scale of 50 MPa, chosen because further offshore the route crosses dense sands.

In one of the tests the final reference reading of the cone resistance is 60 kPa higher than the initial one. The shift happened in the first minutes: the initial reading had been taken before the cone reached the water temperature. The whole profile is therefore 60 kPa too high.

The error in su is the same at every depth: 60 / 15 = 4 kPa. It weighs most where su is smallest, that is near the seabed: at 2 m it is half the true value, at 10 m a fifth. And it goes in the less conservative direction, because the clay comes out stronger than it is.

What the standard says

In the 2012 edition of ISO 22476-1 tests were assigned to four application classes. For cone resistance, class 1 allowed 35 kPa or 5% of the measured value, whichever is larger; class 2 allowed 100 kPa or 5%. In practice the check was made on the zero drift. At 60 kPa the test in the example fell within class 2, which the best practice guideline of the New Zealand CPT contractors describes as a reasonable target in most situations, keeping class 1 for soft to very soft soils.

The 5% exceeds 35 kPa only above 700 kPa of cone resistance, and 100 kPa only above 2 MPa. In the example the corrected cone resistance does not exceed 460 kPa, reached at 10 m: only the fixed threshold in kPa counts, the same at every depth, and its weight on su depends on the soil. Even class 1 leaves at 2 m a possible error of 35 / 15 = 2.3 kPa, 29% of su.

The 2022 edition replaced the application classes with two quantities: the cone penetrometer class, which comes from laboratory calibration, and the test category, which also depends on the zero drift and is known only once the test is over. The thresholds of the current edition are in its Table 3. The mechanism does not change: a threshold in kPa weighs in inverse proportion to the value being measured. The standard covers onshore and nearshore tests, and for offshore ones refers to ISO 19901-8; the example, in 8 m of water, falls within the scope of the former.

What is decided on board

The two readings are known when the cone leaves the ground, with the pontoon still on location. That is the moment when a test can be repeated at the cost of a few hours: the cone is checked and cleaned, left to reach the water temperature, and pushed again a short distance from the first hole. Once the pontoon has demobilised, the same test requires a new mobilisation.

The client representative can take this decision within the day on two conditions: that the threshold is written in advance, in kPa, and that the log with the two readings reaches them at the end of the push, not in the Factual Report. Without the first condition the decision becomes a negotiation; without the second it comes when a repeat costs a campaign.

The objections

The first: the test is within class. It is, and the class says how large the allowed error is, not whether it is acceptable for the use the data will be put to. A threshold in fixed kPa is the same in sand and in clay; in the example class 2 is compatible with a 50% overestimate of su at 2 m.

The second: the drift can be corrected in processing, for instance by spreading it linearly along the test. But nothing says the shift grew linearly, and the two readings do not say when it happened. In the example it happened at the start: a linear correction would remove at 2 m only 12 of the 60 kPa, and su would remain overestimated by 3.2 kPa, 40%. Corrected that way, the test looks better than it is.

The third: the cone is accurate to 0.1% of full scale. That is a common order of magnitude for modern cones, according to Robertson and Cabal. But 0.1% of 50 MPa is 50 kPa, that is 3.3 kPa on su at every depth. And an accuracy measured in the laboratory does not include what happens in the field between the first reading and the second.

The fourth: Nkt is calibrated against laboratory tests. Calibration corrects a ratio, not a constant shift. With a sample at 6 m, an su of 14 kPa and a net cone resistance read as 270 kPa, calibration gives Nkt = 19.3. With that value su is right again at 6 m, but at 2 m it remains overestimated by 17% and at 10 m it becomes underestimated by 7%. The error does not disappear: it changes the gradient of su with depth, which is exactly what the design uses.

What to ask for in the specification

  • 01Both readings, not just the difference. Before and after every test, with their sign, the time and the point where they were taken – at the seabed or at the surface. The standard defines the drift as an absolute value, and an absolute value does not say in which direction the profile is wrong.
  • 02A threshold derived from the use of the data. If at 2 m the design tolerates a 25% error on su, that is 2 kPa, with Nkt of 15 the allowed drift on cone resistance is 30 kPa. It is stricter than class 1 of the 2012 edition, and it is so because the use requires it.
  • 03The stabilisation time before the initial reading. The reading is taken once the cone has reached the water temperature; if the cone measures temperature, it is recorded together with the reading.
  • 04A cone with a full scale suited to the expected soils. If the same route crosses soft clays and dense sands, a lower-capacity cone for the former instead of a 50 MPa cone for everything, and a calibration over the range of values actually measured.
  • 05The repeat rule, decided in advance. Beyond the threshold the test is repeated while the pontoon is on location, and the client representative can call for it without waiting for the report.

The underlying point

A threshold written in kPa looks like a precision requirement, but its weight depends on the soil: in dense sand 60 kPa is 0.6% of the data, in soft clay half the shear strength near the seabed. The two readings that every CPT produces say how far the zero moved while the pontoon is still on location. Reading them there costs a few minutes per test. Reading them in the Factual Report, in the example, leaves in the design a clay stronger than it is.
