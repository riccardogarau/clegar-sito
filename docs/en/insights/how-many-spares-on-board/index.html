How many spares on board: why “one of each” is not a measure

The mobilisation plan declares one spare on board for each critical component, and the specification only asked for “adequate spares for the whole campaign”. The rule looks prudent and the same for everything. For a component installed once, the probability of running out of parts in 42 days is 0.5%. For a module installed twelve times, with the same MTBF, it is 34%.

This article shows what that probability depends on, how to compute it with data the contractor already holds, which objections come up when it is asked for, and what to write into the specification so that “adequate” becomes a number.

What a stop for want of a part depends on

A component stops the work when it fails and there is no spare left on board. The number of failures expected during the campaign depends on three quantities: the failure rate of one unit, how many units are installed, and for how many hours they run. If the rate is constant – the assumption under which an MTBF translates into a rate – one unit fails on average once every MTBF hours, and n identical units n times as often.

The mean number of failures in T operating hours is therefore m = n · T / MTBF. With a constant rate, the number of failures follows the Poisson distribution: the probability of k failures is e−m · mk / k!. With s spares on board, the stop comes at failure number s + 1, and the probability of at least one stop is that of more than s failures: 1 minus the sum of the probabilities of 0 to s failures.

The “one of each” rule sets s = 1 and ignores n. But n enters the calculation with the same weight as the MTBF: twelve units with an MTBF of 10,000 hours fail as often as a single unit with an MTBF of 833 hours.

A worked example

The figures are synthetic – built to illustrate the mechanism, not taken from real projects.

A geophysical survey campaign for a wind farm, 42 days at sea with acquisition around the clock: 1,008 operating hours. Two components declared critical, both with an MTBF of 10,000 hours and one spare on board. The first is the transceiver of the acoustic positioning system, installed once. The second is a digitiser module of the streamer, installed twelve times. The specification in the example does not accept acquisition with a module down: a failed module stops the line.

For the transceiver m = 1 · 1,008 / 10,000 = 0.10 expected failures, and the probability of more than one failure is 0.5%. For the modules m = 12 · 1,008 / 10,000 = 1.21 expected failures: with one spare, the probability of running out of parts at least once is 34.1%.

What a stop costs depends on how long it takes for the part to reach the vessel. In the table every failure without a spare stops acquisition for a fixed time: 2 days for a nearshore site served by a nearby port, 6 days for a distant site, where the part has to be shipped, cleared through customs and taken out to sea. The expected days lost are that time multiplied by the mean number of failures left without a spare.

The level that matters is the campaign

Setting a target per component is not enough. With ten critical components, each with a 5% probability of a stop and failures independent of one another, the probability that the campaign stops at least once for a missing part is 1 − 0.9510 = 40.1%. At 1% per component it falls to 9.6%. It is the same arithmetic as merge bias, already seen for the parallel paths of a schedule: several events must all go well, and the probabilities multiply. The target is set on the campaign and then allocated to the components, not the other way round.

The objections

The first: spares are the contractor's risk, because a breakdown is off-hire. They are for the cost of the day, which the classification of vessel time assigns to the contractor. They are not for the date. The lost day stays lost even if nobody pays for it: the weather window is used up, the next contractor waits, and the milestone that depends on the survey slips. An off-hire stop moves the cost, not the schedule.

The second: the MTBF is a manufacturer's figure, and the contractor cannot do better. The declared MTBF refers to reference conditions, which IEC 61709 describes precisely so that they can be converted to the conditions of use. A towed streamer, wound on and off the winch, does not work in those conditions, and the failure rate at sea is usually higher. The best figure is the contractor's own history: operating hours and failures of the same component on previous campaigns. The calculation copes badly with a wrong input: with an MTBF of 5,000 hours instead of 10,000, three spares leave a 22.5% probability of a stop, and getting below 5% takes five.

The third: the failure rate is not constant. After mobilisation failures are more frequent, from assembly, connectors and handling; towards the end of life they are again, from wear. Both effects raise the number of failures above the constant-rate model, which therefore gives an optimistic estimate, not a cautious one. Quayside tests and the sea trial period also serve to bring early failures out before they count.

The fourth: a module down does not really stop the survey. That depends on what the specification accepts as degraded acquisition. If it accepts one module down, the stop comes one failure later, and with one spare the probability falls from 34.1% to 12.3%: the two-spares row of the table. But that module down is a gap in the data, and it has to be decided beforehand, in the specification, not on board under the pressure of the weather window.

The fifth: spares cost money. True, and the calculation does not deny it: it makes it comparable. The cost of one more spare is set against the expected days lost it avoids – in the example, from 3.05 to 1.00 days going from one to two spares at the distant site – and the decision remains an economic one, but taken with a number instead of a rule.

What to ask for in the specification

  • 01The list of critical components with the units installed. For each, how many the system carries and how many are needed to acquire to specification.
  • 02The failure rate, with its source. The contractor's fleet history – operating hours and failures – where it exists, and the manufacturer's figure only where it does not, declared as such.
  • 03The time for a part to arrive from the site. Not from the warehouse to the port, but from the warehouse to the vessel, for the campaign site and with the planned transfer means.
  • 04A target on the campaign. The maximum probability of a stop for want of a part over the whole campaign, allocated by the contractor to the components.
  • 05Degraded acquisition, written beforehand. How many modules or channels down the specification accepts, for how long and with what effect on the deliverable.
  • 06The calculation in the mobilisation plan. Number of spares per component with the resulting probability, checked at the readiness review against what is actually on board.

The underlying point

“One spare per component” counts components, not failures. Failures depend on how many units are working, for how many hours and at what rate, and the time they cost depends on how far away the warehouse is. These are all figures the contractor has before sailing. Asking for them in the specification turns “adequate spares” into a probability that can be read, discussed and checked at the quayside.
