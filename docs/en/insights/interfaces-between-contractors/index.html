Interfaces between contractors: where they break and who holds them

In the survey contractor's contract, delivery of the route survey report is set for day 120. In the cable installer's contract, the survey data are to be furnished by day 120. The two dates coincide, and neither schedule flags a problem. There is one, and it is worth twenty days.

When a client procures in separate packages – the survey from one contractor, the installation from another – the two contractors have no contract with each other. Each has a contract with the client, written at a different time and by whoever was handling that package. The interface between them exists only where two texts overlap, and neither describes it in full.

The same word in two contracts

An interface rarely breaks because someone is late. It breaks more often because the same word – "delivery", "survey data", "contacts" – means one thing in one contract and another in the other. Each contractor complies with its own text, each schedule is consistent with its own contract, and the difference appears only on the day one passes something to the other.

That is why comparing the dates of the two schedules is not enough. A date says when something is delivered; it does not say whether what is delivered is what the recipient needs. The question to ask of every interface is a different one: on what day does the thing exist in the form the recipient's contract requires?

A worked example

The figures below are illustrative – built to show the mechanism, not taken from real projects – but the contractual structure is a recurring one.

An export cable for a wind farm. Contract A, with the survey contractor, is awarded on day 0. Contract B, with the installer, on day 40. Days are counted from a common origin.

Contract A requires the route survey report to be delivered at Rev A by day 120. The client has ten days for comments, the contractor another ten to issue Rev 0: 120 + 10 + 10 = 140. The report includes the list of boulders of 1.0 m and above within 100 m of the route. The survey contractor's schedule shows Rev A on day 112, eight days before the contractual date.

Contract B, written later, lists among the client-furnished information "the final route survey data", to be provided by day 120, and the boulder clearance plan, which the installer starts on day 150. Its burial tool requires a 20 m wide corridor cleared of boulders of 0.5 m and above.

The third handover runs the other way. The installer completes the lay on day 260 and delivers the as-laid positions within 14 days, that is on day 274. The survey contractor carries out the post-lay survey, mobilises on day 285 and needs those positions three days earlier, on day 282, to plan the lines.

In the first row contract B asks for the final data, and the final data are Rev 0: they exist on day 140, not on day 120. In the second, contract A does not produce what contract B requires at all, because it stops at boulders of 1.0 m. Whoever reads Rev 0 notices on day 140; repeating the contact picking on the existing data with a 0.5 m threshold takes a 25-day variation, and the list arrives on day 165, fifteen days after the clearance plan starts. The second row is the most insidious: on paper it shows thirty days of margin. Only the third is what it seems.

Who is late

On day 120 the survey contractor delivers Rev A, as its contract requires: it is not in default. The installer does not receive the final data its contract promises by that date, and can claim an extension for the delay it suffers as a result. Under contract B the only party that is late is the client.

This is the common mechanism of contracts that list information to be furnished to the contractor: in the 2017 FIDIC Red Book, for instance, delayed drawings and instructions are dealt with under Sub-Clause 1.9, and extension of time under 8.5. What can be claimed, and with what notice, is set by the text of each contract; but the client has no one to pass the delay on to, because the contractor that caused it complied with its own.

Hence the answer to the second question in the title. An interface between two packages is held by the client, because it is the only party that signs both contracts. It is not an organisational choice: nobody else has the means to do it.

The objections that will come

The first: the installer can start on Rev A, which exists on day 120. Technically yes, and it is often worth doing. But contract B does not oblige it to, and if something changes between Rev A and Rev 0, the rework is for the client's account. If the installer is to start on Rev A, that has to be written into contract B, together with the rule for changes between the two revisions.

The second: the survey contractor's schedule shows Rev A on day 112, so there are eight days of margin. They are eight days of the schedule, not of the contract: the contractor can deliver on day 120 without any default. An interface register built on schedule dates counts margins nobody is obliged to keep.

The third: repeating the contact picking at 0.5 m on the existing data is office work, and 25 days is cautious. It depends on how the data were acquired. If the side scan sonar frequency and range, set in contract A for 1.0 m boulders, do not resolve a 0.5 m object, the picking cannot be repeated: a new survey is needed, with a vessel to remobilise. The example assumes the favourable case, in which the data are enough.

The same register, on day 40

The day to build the register is the award of the second contract: it is the first day on which both texts exist, and the last on which correcting one or the other does not cost a variation on work already under way.

On day 40 the survey contractor has not yet acquired a metre of data. Adding the 0.5 m threshold to its scope, and checking that the sonar configuration allows it, means receiving the 0.5 m boulders with Rev 0 on day 140: ten days before they are needed, instead of fifteen after. For the first row the choice is between two definitions – Rev A on day 120 with a rule for changes, or Rev 0 on day 140 – and the installer's schedule shows which of the two holds. Either way, the date written into contract B is the one on which the thing actually exists.

What to require in interface management

  • 01An interface register held by the client. Opened at the award of the second contract and updated with every variation. No contractor can hold it in the client's place, because none of them sees the other contract.
  • 02The definition of what is handed over, quoted from both contracts. Word for word: revision, content, thresholds, format. The gap is found by comparing the two quotations, not by comparing the dates.
  • 03Contractual dates, not schedule dates. The margin a contractor keeps in its own schedule is not an obligation, and must not be counted as margin on the interface.
  • 04The review cycle counted as a duration. Client comments and reissue are calendar days: in the example they put twenty days between delivery and the final data.
  • 05An owner for every interface, on the client's side. A person who knows both contracts and whose job is to compare them, not to forward documents.

The underlying point

Two schedules each consistent with its own contract can be incompatible with each other, and neither shows it. The incompatibility lies in the words, not in the dates: finding it means reading the two texts side by side.

In the example, two interfaces out of three were late without any contractor being in default. The delay was already written on day 40, and the client was the only party able to read it.

References

  • FIDIC, Conditions of Contract for Construction (Red Book), 2017 edition – Sub-Clause 1.9 on delayed drawings or instructions, Sub-Clause 8.5 on extension of time for completion.
