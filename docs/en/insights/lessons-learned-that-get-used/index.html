The lessons learned nobody rereads, and how to write a note that will be used

At the end of a campaign the lessons learned register carries one line: "Acquisition took 31% longer than planned: on future projects increase acquisition durations by 31%." It is true, it is measured, and applied to the next project it is three days out.

Lessons learned have two faults, and usually only the first is discussed: nobody rereads them. The second costs more, because it looks like correct use. A note that records an outcome – a percentage, a delay, a cost – is, when reread, applied to a project other than the one it came from, and it carries the geometry of the first one with it.

Why the register is not reread

The register is filled in at close-out, while whoever ran the campaign still knows what happened. The decision the note ought to change – the duration in the schedule, the production rate in a tender evaluation – is taken months later, often by other people, in another document. Whoever prepares the next tender does not open the archive: they open their own duration model.

The pattern is the one also described by PMI's PMBOK Guide, in its sixth edition: the lessons learned register is an output of the Manage Project Knowledge process, and at project closure its content is transferred to the organisation's repository. The step no register guarantees is the return trip: from the repository to the document in which the next decision is taken.

A worked example

The figures below are illustrative – built to show the mechanism, not taken from real projects – but the mechanism appears every time a note records a percentage.

Project 1: a geophysical survey of an area with a towed system, side scan sonar and sub-bottom profiler. Sixty lines of 10 km, 600 line km in all, acquired at 4 knots, that is 7.408 km/h: 600 / 7.408 = 81.0 hours on line. The schedule assumed one line turn per line, of 15 minutes. At sea each took 45: with the system in the water the vessel turns wide, and after the turn a run-in is needed for the towfish to settle in height and heading before start of line.

The durations exclude weather and breakdowns, which the daily report records under their own headings. The planned duration was 81.0 + 60 × 0.25 = 96.0 hours; the actual one 81.0 + 60 × 0.75 = 126.0 hours. Thirty hours more, 31%: that is the line that goes into the register.

Project 2, a year later: same system, same water depth, same length of cable out, but an area of a different shape. There are 240 lines of 2.5 km: still 600 km, still 81.0 hours on line. The duration model still uses 15 minutes per turn, and the planner applies the note from project 1.

With the percentage note, the forecast duration of project 2 is 141.0 × 1.31 = 184.7 hours. The actual one is 261.0: 76.3 hours short, 3.2 days of vessel time at continuous operations. The same lesson written another way – "thirty extra minutes per turn" – gives 141.0 + 240 × 0.5 = 261.0 hours: the exact duration.

Why the percentage does not transfer

Acquisition duration is the sum of two terms: time on line, which depends on the kilometres, and turn time, which depends on the number of lines. The error on project 1 lay entirely in the second term. The percentage divides it by the total duration, and so ties it to the geometry of that project: 31% holds only where turns weigh as much as they did there.

On project 1 the turns were 16% of the planned duration, on project 2 43%. For the same kilometres, lines a quarter as long multiply the turns by four, and with them the hours lost. The percentage note was not wrong: it measured a ratio in which only one of the two terms carries over from one project to the next.

The objections that will come

The first: 45 minutes holds for that vessel and that system, not for the next contractor. True, and that is why the note records the conditions under which the value was measured. It is not copied into another contractor's bid: it is used to ask them a question – what turn time do you assume, and with how much cable out? – and to recognise a 15-minute value when it appears. If on the next project the water is shallower and the cable out shorter, the run-in shortens too: with a per-turn note it can be corrected, with a percentage it cannot.

The second: the contractor knows its turn times, and they are its risk. That depends on the contract. On a day rate, the extra hours are paid by the client. On a lump sum the contractor has priced them, but at tender the client also compares durations: a bid assuming 15-minute turns looks shorter than one assuming 45, and may win for the wrong reason.

The third: lines can be run skipping one at each turn, so that the vessel turns with the wide radius the towed system needs without having to sail a loop. True, and it changes the turn time. That is why the note also records the pattern in which the lines were run: a value measured with one pattern cannot be compared with a plan that uses another.

How to write a note that will be used

The first fault – nobody rereads – is corrected by where the note ends up, not by a tidier register. A note is read if it sits in the document in which the next decision is taken: the parameter table of the duration model, the tender evaluation checklist, the specification. The second fault is corrected by its form: a parameter, not an outcome.

Rewritten that way, the line from project 1 becomes: turn time with a towed system, including run-in; planned 15 minutes, measured 45, mean of the 60 turns taken from the end-of-line and start-of-line times; valid for the cable out, speed and line pattern of the project, which the note records; to be entered in the parameter table of the duration model, by a person named in the note.

What to require

  • 01Every note written as a parameter, not as an outcome. The planned value, the measured one and the unit it refers to: per turn, per line, per sample, per day. An outcome expressed as a percentage carries the geometry of the project that produced it.
  • 02The conditions the value depends on. System, cable out, water depth, line pattern: everything that, if it changes, makes the value no longer comparable.
  • 03The document the note goes into. The duration model, the tender checklist, the specification. A note with no place in the document of the next decision stays in the archive.
  • 04In the request for tender, the production rate broken down. Acquisition speed, turn time including run-in, number of lines. A production rate given in kilometres per day hides the second item.
  • 05In the daily report, the start-of-line and end-of-line times. Without them turn time cannot be measured, and the note for the next project goes back to being an impression.

The underlying point

A lesson is useful if it changes a future decision, and a future decision is taken on a different project. The 31% was an exact figure for project 1; carried over, it was 76 hours out. The same error, written as thirty extra minutes per turn, gave the right duration for project 2.

The question to ask of every line in the register, before closing it, is therefore this: on a project with a different geometry, would whoever reads it know what to change, and in which document?
