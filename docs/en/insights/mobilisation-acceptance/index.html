Mobilisation acceptance, item by item: the checks that cannot fail

At the end of mobilisation the client representative signs an acceptance sheet: one row per item, a difference, a tolerance, an outcome. When every row says OK, the vessel sails. No row says whether that check could have produced a different outcome.

Each item compares the system with a reference. For the comparison to say anything, two conditions are needed, and the sheet records neither: the reference must be independent of the error being sought, and it must be more precise than the tolerance. Without the first, the item passes whatever the error. Without the second, the outcome depends more on the reading than on the system.

Why it is all decided on the quayside

Once the vessel has sailed, quality control compares the data with itself: cross lines with main lines, one pass with its reciprocal. These comparisons find the errors that change from one line to the next. A constant error – the same on every line, for the whole campaign – appears identically in both terms of every difference and cancels. The crossline check does not see it, by construction.

Mobilisation acceptance is the moment when a constant error is cheapest to find, and often the only one, because it is the only time the system is compared with something external. If the check there cannot fail, the error sails with the vessel and ends up in the deliverable.

A worked example

The figures below are illustrative – built to show the mechanism, not taken from real projects – but the proportions are those of a real campaign.

A multibeam survey ahead of dredging a port approach channel: 2000 m long by 150 m wide, 300,000 m² in all. Depths are reduced to the tide measured by a gauge in the port, so the transducer draft enters the calculation: the distance between the water surface and the face of each transducer. At sailing, the acceptance sheet reads:

Three items, three passes. The third is the one the dredging volume depends on, and it is worth re-reading with one question: what does the single-beam have in common with the multibeam?

The reference that shares the error

A transducer's draft is not measured directly. The dimensional control survey gives the height of each transducer relative to a reference point on the hull; alongside, the freeboard – the distance between that point and the water surface – is then measured, and from that single reading the drafts of all the transducers are derived.

In the example the vessel has a beam of 11.5 m and the freeboard is read with a tape on one side only. With the mobilisation equipment loaded on the other side, the vessel lists 1.0° away from the side where the reading is taken. The side being read is 5.75 m from the centreline, and at that distance a list of 1.0° raises it by 5.75 × tan 1° = 0.10 m. The freeboard read is 0.10 m greater than at the centre of the vessel, where the transducers are: the drafts come out 0.10 m too small, and every depth 0.10 m too shallow.

The single-beam takes its draft from the same reading. The two echo sounders are therefore wrong by the same amount, in the same direction, and their difference – 0.03 m, within tolerance – contains their individual mounting and measurement errors, but not the common one. The item was carried out correctly, and it could not fail for the error that matters.

What 0.10 m costs

Across the whole channel the seabed lies above the design level, so a uniform vertical shift changes the dredging volume by exactly area times shift: 300,000 m² × 0.10 m = 30,000 m³. The survey shows the seabed on average 0.80 m above the design level, or 240,000 m³; the real seabed is 0.70 m above it, or 210,000 m³. The survey volume exceeds the real one by 14%.

30,000 m³of dredging that exist only in the survey

If the pre-dredge survey is the basis of the contract quantities, those 30,000 m³ go into the tender and the schedule, and someone pays for them without their existing. The acceptance sheet that should have stopped them says OK.

The reference less precise than the tolerance

The obvious remedy is a reference that does not go through the freeboard: a direct measurement of the depth below the transducers. There are two common ones, and this is where the second condition comes in. The lead line – a weight on a graduated line – is independent, but alongside, with some current and the reading taken at the water surface, its uncertainty in the example is 0.05 m: as large as the tolerance. A bar lowered beneath the transducers to a depth marked on its cable, with the echo read by the echo sounders themselves, has an uncertainty of 0.02 m in the example.

With a real error of 0.10 m and a tolerance of 0.05 m, the probability that the item comes out OK depends entirely on the reference. In the table the uncertainties are standard deviations, and reading errors follow a normal distribution:

ReferenceUncertainty of the comparison (m)OK with the 0.10 m errorOK with no error
Single-beam, same freeboard reading0.0299%99%

The single-beam gives the same answer with the error and without it: it does not see it. The lead line sees it, but poorly: it lets the wrong transducer through about one time in six and fails the correct one nearly one time in three. Only the bar separates the two cases.

And there is an effect the table does not show. An item that fails is not closed with a rejection: it is measured again. If each measurement is independent of the previous one, with the lead line the transducer that is 0.10 m out passes by the third attempt four times in ten. The sheet records the last attempt, not how many there were.

The objections that will come

The first: the comparison between multibeam and single-beam is not meant to verify the draft, but to verify that the multibeam measures like a simpler echo sounder. True, and that is exactly the point. That item verifies the transducers, not their draft; but on the sheet it is the only vertical item, it is headed "depth", and whoever reads it takes it for the depth check.

The second: depths are reduced with GNSS heights, so the draft does not enter the calculation. In that case the freeboard reading really does leave the calculation, and with it the error in the example; in its place comes the vertical distance between antenna and transducers, taken from the dimensional control survey. The question stays the same, though, and must be put to the item that replaces it: what is it compared with, and what does that have in common with what it verifies.

The third: the volume paid for is the difference between the pre-dredge and post-dredge surveys, and a constant error cancels in the difference. It cancels if the two surveys have the same error: same vessel, same freeboard reading, same list. A new mobilisation is a new reading, and the two surveys are not infrequently carried out by different parties. And before any post-dredge survey exists, the volume of the first has already set the tender.

What to require in the specification

  • 01For each item, what the reference has in common with the system. One more column on the acceptance sheet. Where the answer is not "nothing", the item does not verify the common error, and the sheet must say so.
  • 02The uncertainty of the reference next to the tolerance. A reference as uncertain as the tolerance produces an outcome that depends more on the reading than on the system. The ratio between the two is set before mobilisation, not in front of the result.
  • 03Every attempt recorded, not just the last. An item passed at the third attempt is different information from one passed at the first.
  • 04A vertical check that does not go through the freeboard. A bar lowered beneath the transducers, or a comparison with a known level; and the freeboard read on both sides, with the list recorded.
  • 05Tolerances derived from the use of the data. On the channel in the example, a depth tolerance of 0.05 m is worth 15,000 m³. If that volume is too much, the tolerance is too loose, whatever the generic procedure says.

The underlying point

An acceptance sheet lists items passed. Of each one it is worth asking whether it could have failed: whether the reference was independent of the error sought, and precise enough to see it. An item that could not fail is not a check neglected by whoever was on the quay; it is a badly designed check, and has been since the day the procedure was approved.

Constant errors are precisely the ones that later quality control does not find. They are found on the quayside, against an external reference, or someone else finds them, later and at a different cost.

References
