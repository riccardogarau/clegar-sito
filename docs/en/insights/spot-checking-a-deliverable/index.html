Spot checks: what a sample with no errors actually says

A list of 2,400 seabed contacts, delivered with the interpretation report of a survey for a wind farm. In the quality control section the contractor writes that 30 were verified, by rechecking their class and height, and no errors were found. The sentence is true, and it says less than it seems: that result is still compatible, at 95% confidence, with a list in which one contact in ten is wrong.

This article shows what a spot check proves and what it does not, why a sample expressed as a percentage of the lot does not guarantee a percentage of errors, which objections come up when this is pointed out, and what to write into the specification so that the client's check says something.

What a sample with no errors proves

If a share p of the contacts in a list is wrong, and n of them are checked at random, the probability of finding none wrong is (1 − p)n. A sample with no errors rules out, at 95% confidence, only the shares for which that probability falls below 5%. The highest share still compatible is the upper bound: 1 − 0.051/n.

The bound is roughly 3/n. This is the “rule of three”, described by Hanley and Lippman-Hand in 1983 to interpret clinical studies in which the event of concern had never occurred. With 30 contacts the bound is 9.5%: the check found no errors, but it did not rule out that the list holds 228 of them.

The calculation treats the lot as if it were infinite, and for a finite lot it is conservative. If exactly 48 of 2,400 contacts were wrong, a sample of 149 would find none with a probability of 4.5%, not 4.9%: 144 would be enough to show the same threshold. The margin is small as long as the sample is small compared with the lot.

A worked example

The figures are synthetic – built to illustrate the mechanism, not taken from real projects.

A geophysical survey of a wind farm site, with side scan sonar and multibeam. The deliverable is a list of 2,400 contacts interpreted as boulders, each with position, height and class. The list is used to choose the foundation positions and to size the boulder clearance campaign. The client decides it can accept up to 2% wrong contacts, that is 48. A contact is wrong if an independent interpreter, working on the same data, assigns it a different class, or measures a height that differs by more than 0.3 m.

5% is not a sampling plan

In specifications the spot check is often written as a percentage: the client verifies 5% of the items delivered. But the bound depends on how many items are checked, and hardly at all on how many there are in the lot. On a list of 600 contacts 5% is 30, and the bound stays at 9.5%. On 2,400 it is 120, and the bound falls to 2.5%. On 12,000 it is 600, and it falls to 0.5%.

Multiplying each bound by its own lot, the three cases give almost the same number: 57, 59 and 60 wrong contacts still possible. A 5% sample with no errors limits the number of errors in the lot – roughly 3 divided by 0.05 – not their share. If the use of the data calls for a share, the sample size is derived from the share, not from the lot.

What the standards say

Sampling by attributes has two ISO standards. ISO 2859-1, whose third edition dates from 2026, indexes its plans by acceptance quality limit (AQL) and is designed for a continuing series of lots from the same supplier: the client is protected by the rules that switch to tighter inspection when quality worsens from one lot to the next. A list of contacts delivered once has no next lot to apply them to.

For isolated lots there is ISO 2859-2, in its 2020 edition, which indexes its plans by limiting quality (LQ): the share of defectives the client wants a low probability of accepting. For its plans that probability, the consumer's risk, is usually below 10%. It is the question of the example asked from the right side: not how many errors the supplier may have on average, but which lot the client does not want to accept. With a 10% risk instead of 5%, the 2% threshold is shown by 114 contacts with no errors instead of 149.

The objections

The first: the contractor chose the sample, among the most representative contacts. The calculation holds only for a sample drawn at random; a chosen sample, whoever chooses it, says something about the contacts chosen and nothing about the list. The client makes the draw, after delivery, over the whole list, and records how it was made – the generator and the seed – so that anyone can repeat it.

The second: errors found are corrected. Correcting them fixes those contacts, not the estimate for the rest. If 2 wrong contacts are found among 149, the estimate for the list is 1.3%, that is 32 contacts: with the 2 found corrected, about 30 remain. And the upper bound rises to 4.2%, above the threshold. The list is not shown to be acceptable, and what gets reworked is the process that produced it – the interpreter, the area, the criterion – not the two contacts.

The third: errors are not spread at random, they cluster. Often true, and the bound concerns the whole list, not its parts. If 600 contacts lie in an area of sand waves, where boulders are confused with crests, and 6% of them are wrong there against 0.5% elsewhere, errors over the whole list are 1.9%: below the threshold. A random sample of 149 contacts takes on average 37 from that area, and about one time in ten finds no error there. If there are foundations in that area, the area is a lot of its own, with its own sample.

The fourth: the check verified the list. A sample drawn from the list finds the wrong contacts that are in it, not the boulders that are missing. For omissions the sample is drawn from the area: cells chosen at random, reinterpreted from scratch by an independent interpreter, and compared with the list. The arithmetic is the same; what is counted changes.

The fifth: 149 contacts are too many. If rechecking a contact takes three minutes, 149 contacts are less than eight hours of work, against a boulder clearance campaign sized on that list. And the number does not grow with the lot: a list of 12,000 contacts would need 148.

What to ask for in the specification

  • 01What counts as an error, written in advance. A class different from that of an independent interpreter, a height that differs by more than a threshold, a position off by more than a given distance: measurable criteria, not a judgement made once the sample is finished.
  • 02The share to be shown and the accepted risk. The 2% at 95% of the example, or the limiting quality and consumer's risk in the terms of ISO 2859-2. The sample size is derived from there: 149 contacts with no errors, not 5% of the list.
  • 03A random draw made by the client. After delivery, over the whole lot, with the generator and the seed recorded so that the draw can be repeated.
  • 04One lot for each use. Areas where the data decides something different – the foundation positions, a cable route – are sampled separately.
  • 05Two samples, not one. One from the list, for wrong contacts; one from the area, for missing boulders.
  • 06The rejection rule, decided in advance. If the bound exceeds the threshold the lot is reworked and sampled again from scratch. Correcting the items found in the sample is not enough.

The underlying point

A spot check with no errors looks like proof of quality, but it says only which error shares it has ruled out. With 30 contacts it has ruled out little: up to 9.5% remains possible. The sample size is derived from what the client wants to show, not from a percentage of the lot, and the draw is made by whoever has to rely on the result. Written that way into the specification, the same check costs a day and says whether the list can be used.
