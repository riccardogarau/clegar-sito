A stale sound velocity profile: recognising it in the data, not by the clock

At six in the morning the contractor lowers the probe and measures the sound velocity profile. The specification asks for a profile every twelve hours, and the probe mounted at the transducer measures sound velocity continuously at that depth: all day long it reads the same value as the profile. At four in the afternoon a new profile shows that the water below changed hours ago. In the data from those ten hours, over a flat seabed, the edge of the swath and the centre are 0.38 m apart.

The profile complied with the specification, and the probe raised no alarm. This article shows why a profile goes stale without the probe at the transducer noticing, how to recognise it in the data rather than by the clock, what can be recovered in processing and what to ask for in the specification.

What the profile is for

For each beam a multibeam measures a two-way travel time and a launch angle. To turn them into a point on the seabed, processing reconstructs the path of the sound through the water column, layer by layer: where the velocity changes the beam bends, and the resulting point depends on where the profile says the velocity changes. At nadir the beam goes straight down and does not bend: a wrong profile only gets the mean velocity wrong, and therefore the depth scale. An outer beam crosses the thermocline at a slant and bends, and a thermocline placed at the wrong depth gets its curve wrong as well as its velocity.

The article on witnessing a calibration showed that this error is symmetric about nadir, and that two reciprocal passes separate it from roll. The question here is a different one: not how to isolate it in a one-hour test, but how a survey lasting weeks notices it while the water changes.

A worked example

The figures are synthetic – built to illustrate the mechanism, not taken from real projects.

Flat seabed at 40 m, transducers at 4 m draught, usable beams out to 60 degrees from nadir: about 60 m each side. The water column has two layers: above the thermocline sound travels at 1510 m/s, below it at 1490 m/s. The 06:00 profile puts the thermocline at 12 m. During the day the wind mixes the surface layer and the thermocline sinks by one metre an hour, reaching 22 m at 16:00. The velocity above the thermocline does not change: at transducer depth it stays at 1510 m/s.

After ten hours the flat seabed comes out of processing curved: 13 cm shallower than the truth at the centre, 25 cm deeper at the edges. Every line of the afternoon has it, with the same sign and the same shape.

Why the probe does not see it

The probe at the transducer measures sound velocity at a single point: the depth at which it is mounted. The multibeam needs it to form the beams at launch, and it is a good alarm when the water at the surface changes – rain, river water, the warming of a calm day. In the example it is the depth of the thermocline that changes, not the velocity above it: the probe reads 1510 m/s at 06:00 and 1510 m/s at 16:00, in agreement with the profile all day. Comparing probe and profile checks the water at transducer depth, not the thirty-six metres below.

Nor does the twelve-hour rule see it. A fixed interval assumes that the water changes at a known pace; a thermocline sinking a metre an hour, or a tidal front passing through, does not keep to it. S-44 does not set how often a profile must be measured: it sets the allowed vertical uncertainty, and leaves it to whoever writes the specification to decide how it is met.

How to recognise it in the data

A stale profile leaves a signature in the data with three features. Taken together, they set it apart from other causes.

It is symmetric. The starboard and port edges move in the same direction. A roll error moves them in opposite directions.

It grows with the hours since the profile, not with position. Measuring line by line, over stretches of flat seabed, the difference between the mean depth of the outer beams and that of the beams near nadir, in the example it rises by about 4 cm an hour.

It drops back to zero when a new profile comes in. The seabed does not know what time the profile was measured: if the edge-minus-nadir difference falls abruptly on the first line processed with the new profile, the cause was the water.

None of the three needs extra equipment: the data already acquired and the time of the profile applied to each line are enough. With a threshold of 0.15 m on the edge-minus-nadir difference, in the example a new profile would have been called for in the fourth hour, with the edge error at 0.10 m rather than 0.25.

The objections

The first: the seabed is not flat. That is true almost everywhere, and it is why the check is made on the stretches where it is, and read over time rather than as a single value. Morphology changes with position, not with hours since the profile, and it does not drop back to zero when a new one comes in. Where flat stretches are missing, the same comparison can be made in the overlap between adjacent lines acquired at different hours since the profile: the same seabed, seen by the outer beams of both, must give the same depth.

The second: the error is within TVU. For Order 1a it is: after ten hours the edge error is 35% of the allowed TVU. But TVU is the budget for every source – positioning, tide, vessel motion, transducer draught – and this one, on its own, is worth a third of it. Nor is it a random error that averaging many beams reduces: it has the same sign on every line of a whole afternoon, and a seabed model inherits all of it. For Special Order, the same profile takes the edge error to 64% of the TVU.

The third: the crosslines will find it. They find it if they were acquired in different water from the lines they cross, and only at the crossing points; reduced to a percentage of crossings within tolerance, as the article on the crossline check showed, they can hide it. The signature described above can be read on every line, the same day.

The fourth: it can be corrected in processing. Often it can, which is one more reason to notice early. Reprocessing the data with another profile does not require going back to sea, provided the beam travel times and angles have been kept and provided there is a profile that describes the water at those hours. In the example, in the fifth hour, the edge error is +0.12 m with the 06:00 profile and −0.12 m with the 16:00 one: the two profiles are equally far away in time, and choosing the nearer one does not help. Interpolating between them brings the error below one centimetre.

Interpolation works here because the thermocline sank steadily. Had a front passed through in an hour, there would have been nothing to interpolate between two profiles ten hours apart. Data are recovered with the profiles that were measured, not with the ones that could have been.

What to ask for in the specification

  • 01A criterion on the data, as well as on the clock. A new profile when the edge-minus-nadir difference over a flat stretch exceeds a stated threshold, when the probe at the transducer departs from the profile, and in any case within a maximum interval.
  • 02The profile applied to each line, recorded with its time. Without it, nobody can check afterwards which water a line was processed with.
  • 03The profile signature in the daily QC. The edge-minus-nadir difference, line by line, against hours since the profile, separately for starboard and port. It is a chart, and it takes a minute to read while the vessel is still on site.
  • 04A profile before and one after each block of lines. The end-of-block profile costs a few minutes and makes interpolation possible; without it, the data from those hours can only be corrected with an assumption about the water.
  • 05Beam travel times and angles kept, together with the raw profiles. Correction in processing is possible only if the raw data still exist when it turns out to be needed.

The underlying point

A sound velocity profile describes the water of one hour. The fixed interval says when it was measured, the probe at the transducer says whether the water at the surface has changed; neither says whether the water below is still the same. The data say it, carrying the shape of the wrong profile on every line: all it takes is to ask that someone looks at it while the vessel is still on site.
