Line spacing: the strip where a boulder goes unseen at 100% coverage

The specification asks for every boulder at least 0.5 m high to be identified across a block intended for wind farm foundations, with side scan sonar at 100% coverage. The final report states the coverage was achieved, and the map confirms it: no gaps between one line and the next. Yet a fifth of the block was seen only under conditions in which a boulder of that height is hard to recognise. That part is not between the lines, but beneath them.

The coverage percentage says how much of the seabed came within the sonar's range at least once. It does not say at what distance from the line, and distance is what decides whether an object of a given height can be seen. This article shows where the strip that coverage counts as seen lies, how wide it is, why tightening the lines does not remove it, and what to write in the specification so that someone covers it.

A low boulder is recognised by its shadow

Side scan sonar is towed a few metres above the seabed and looks sideways. A boulder returns a strong echo, but so does coarse seabed: what sets it apart is the shadow it casts on the side away from the sonar, and the length of that shadow also gives its height. With the towfish at altitude H above the seabed and an object of height h at horizontal distance x from the point beneath the towfish, on a flat seabed, the shadow is:

L = x × h / (H − h)

The shadow grows in proportion to the distance from the line. Far from the sonar the sound arrives at a grazing angle and the shadow is long; close to the vertical it comes down steeply and the shadow shortens until it almost disappears. Five metres from the line, the shadow of a half-metre boulder is 36 cm long; at the edge of the range it exceeds 5 m. The same boulder, in the same survey, is obvious or nearly invisible depending on the line it is seen from.

A worked example

The figures below are synthetic – built to illustrate the mechanism, not taken from real projects – but the geometry is that of any side scan survey.

A block of 2.8 × 4 km, water depths between 25 and 45 m. Side scan with a range of 75 m per side, towfish 7.5 m above the seabed, speed 4 knots. In the tender the contractor stated that its interpreter reliably recognises a 0.5 m boulder when the shadow is at least 1 m long. By the formula, the shadow reaches 1 m at 14 m from the line: within 14 m on each side, that is in a strip 28 m wide centred on every line, the line itself does not meet the criterion.

The contractor plans lines 140 m apart. The swaths of two adjacent lines, 150 m wide each, overlap by 10 m, and coverage is complete. But the strip beneath each line is 140 m from the neighbouring lines, almost twice their range: no other line sees it.

20%of the block seen only from a distance at which a 0.5 m boulder casts a shadow shorter than 1 m, at 100% coverage

That is 28 m in every 140, or 2.24 km² out of 11.2.

Tightening the lines is not enough

The instinctive reaction is to reduce the spacing. The table shows what happens:

At 100 m the share of the block seen only from close goes up instead of down. The reason is geometric: the strip beneath one line is seen by another line only if that line is within its range, that is no more than 75 m away. As long as the spacing stays above the range, bringing the lines closer does not let anyone see the strip from further away: it adds strips, one per line, still 28 m wide, over shorter intervals.

Below 75 m the problem disappears at once. With lines 70 m apart, every point in the block lies more than 14 m from at least one line that has it within range: even the point directly beneath a line is 70 m from both neighbours, where the shadow of a 0.5 m boulder is 5 m long. The 5 m margin between spacing and range absorbs the towfish's lateral offset from the planned track. In practice it is 200% coverage, and it costs 164 line km instead of 84: 95% more.

The contractor's objections

The first: the edge of the range is the worst part of the sonar, and at 70 m a small boulder gets lost. That has to be checked, not assumed. With a 75 m range the sonar can transmit at most 10 pulses per second, because before transmitting the next it has to wait for the echo from the edge; at 4 knots the vessel moves 0.21 m between one pulse and the next. With a horizontal beam of 0.3 degrees, the value of the sonar in the example, the footprint at 70 m is 0.37 m wide, and a 0.5 m boulder is hit by 4 consecutive pulses. In this configuration the edge holds; at higher speeds or with longer ranges the calculation has to be redone.

The second: the 1 m criterion is conservative, and an experienced interpreter recognises shorter shadows. Perhaps, and then the strip narrows but does not disappear: with a minimum shadow of 0.5 m the limit falls to 7 m from the line, the strip to 14 m and, with lines 140 m apart, the share of the block seen only from close to 10%. The criterion decides how wide the strip is, not whether it exists, and that is why it has to be written down: a contractor who states it in the tender commits to a spacing, one who does not, does not.

The third is the most serious: the vessel runs along the same line, and the multibeam sees exactly the strip beneath the track. That is true, and it is a legitimate solution, on two conditions. The first is that the specification says so: if the multibeam is what identifies boulders in the strip, its data has to be examined for that purpose and against a criterion, not just gridded for bathymetry. The second is that the multibeam can do it at the depth of the block. Directly beneath the vessel, the seabed footprint of a 1 degree beam is 0.44 m wide at 25 m depth and 0.79 m at 45 m: in the deeper part of the block the boulder is smaller than the footprint that is supposed to reveal it. That does not mean it cannot be seen; it means it has to be demonstrated, for example on a known object at the maximum depth, rather than taken for granted.

What the standard says

Many offshore survey specifications borrow the vocabulary of IHO S-44, which is written for safety of navigation. The standard keeps three quantities apart: feature detection, the ability of a system to detect objects of a defined size; feature search, the extent of the area surveyed with a systematic method of identifying objects; and bathymetric coverage. Of 100% coverage it states that it does not guarantee continuous depth measurements. Of detection ability it says that the entire system has to be assessed – equipment, methods, procedures and personnel – not the instrument alone. And for Exclusive Order, the most demanding, it requires a 200% feature search, which can be achieved by adequately overlapping the lines.

A coverage percentage measures how much area has been run over, not what would have been seen there. The standard keeps the two apart; a specification that says only “100% coverage” merges them into one.

What to ask for in the specification

  • 01The object to be identified, with the recognition criterion. The target size and, for side scan, the minimum shadow length or the number of pulses on the object that the interpreter considers reliable. Without a criterion the spacing cannot be checked.
  • 02Spacing derived from the criterion, not from the range. The contractor states at what distance from the line the criterion stops being met, and how the remaining strip is covered: by another line that has it within range, or by another instrument.
  • 03If the multibeam covers the strip, state it and demonstrate it. With a detection criterion for the multibeam and a test on the target at the maximum depth of the block, not with gridded bathymetry alone.
  • 04Towfish altitude and speed among the controlled parameters. Altitude decides the width of the strip, speed the number of pulses on the object at the edge of the range. Outside the declared values the calculation has to be redone.
  • 05A detectability map as well as a coverage map. For each cell, the greatest distance within range from which it was seen. Cells seen only from close are the strip in this article, and on a map they stand out at a glance.

The underlying point

Coverage answers the question “did the sonar pass over here?”. The specification needs the answer to a different one: “from here, would a 0.5 m boulder have been seen?”. In the example the two answers agree over 80% of the block and diverge over the 20% that lies exactly beneath the lines, where intuition says the data should be best.
