A technical opinion in a dispute: what makes it usable

The contractor claims 50 hours of spread time for the plough's lower progress through a section of route with boulders. The client's expert answers that the hours are 20. Before the arbitral tribunal there are two figures drawn from the same records, with the same method, and neither report says why they differ.

A technical opinion in a dispute does not serve whoever commissions it: it serves whoever has to decide, and whoever decides is usually not a technician. An opinion that reaches a figure without showing the choice the figure depends on leaves the tribunal with two numbers and no criterion for preferring one. This article takes a lost productivity case and shows where that choice lies, how much it weighs, and how to write an opinion that puts it on the table instead of hiding it.

The method is the same, the reference is not

To quantify lost productivity, one of the most widely used methods is the measured mile: progress is measured over a period or section not disturbed by the disputed event, it is compared with progress in the disturbed section, and the difference, converted into hours, is the loss. AACE Recommended Practice 25R-03 describes it as a comparison between impacted and unimpacted periods of similar work. The last two words do all the work.

The method does not say which section is the reference. Whoever does the calculation chooses it, and if the chosen section differs from the disturbed one in anything besides the disputed event, that difference ends up inside the loss and is attributed to the event. Two experts correctly applying the same method to the same records reach different figures because they chose different references. If the reports do not say so, the dispute looks like an argument about method, when it is in fact an argument about a stretch of cable.

A worked example

The figures below are illustrative – built to show the mechanism, not taken from real projects – but the pattern recurs in every claim for lost productivity.

Between KP 18 and KP 30 the plough meets boulders which the contractor maintains were unforeseeable, and it claims compensation for the lower progress. Ploughing hours are counted the same way in every section: from entry to exit, stops for boulder removal included, weather stops excluded.

With the two clay sections taken together, 12,000 m in 55 hours, the loss attributable to the boulders is 25 hours: half the claim. The other half is the difference in progress between sand and clay, which has nothing to do with the boulders. Taken one at a time, the clay sections give 20 and 30 hours. That range, not a single figure, is the result a usable opinion delivers, together with the reason the sand reference falls outside it.

The cross-check

A result drawn from a comparison between sections can be checked against a fact that does not depend on the comparison. The daily reports record 17 hours of plough stops for boulder removal between KP 18 and KP 30. They are a floor, not the total: boulders slow the plough between one stop and the next as well. The range of 20 to 30 hours sits above that floor, as it should. The 50 hours claimed would mean that, on top of the 17 hours of stops, the boulders slowed progress by another 33 hours – while the difference in soil explains that part without any need for the boulders.

The objections that will come

The first, from the contractor: the clay between KP 18 and KP 30 is stiffer than between KP 32 and KP 44, so the clay reference is too favourable. It is a question of fact, and the opinion treats it as one: it compares the site investigation data in the three sections and says whether they tell them apart. If they do not, the objection remains an assertion. If they do, the range widens – but any clay reference gives progress far from the 400 m/h of the sand, and the conclusion stands: a substantial part of the claim does not depend on the boulders.

The second comes from the other side, and is a limit of the clay reference: the sections between KP 32 and KP 44 were ploughed after the boulder section, by a crew with more practice in that ground. If the learning effect is there, it makes the reference faster and the calculated loss higher, so it works in favour of the claimant. The opinion states it, with the direction of the error: an uncertainty stated with its direction can be weighed; one left unsaid is discovered at the hearing.

The third: the measured mile is a recognised method, so the contractor's result is correct. The method is recognised, and that is exactly why the argument should not be about the method. The result depends on how similar the compared sections are, which is not a property of the method but a choice of whoever applies it, and it has to be justified like any other input to the calculation.

What the opinion does not decide

The opinion does not establish whether the boulders were foreseeable, nor who bears the ground risk: those are contractual questions, and the tribunal decides them. The opinion provides the figures for each possible answer. If the boulder risk lies with the client, the loss is 20 to 30 hours. If the contractor maintains that the clay too was unforeseeable, that is a different claim, on a different contractual basis, and has to be proven separately – it cannot be brought in through the door of the measured mile.

The IBA Rules on the Taking of Evidence in International Arbitration require a party-appointed expert's report to contain, among other things, a description of the instructions received, the facts on which it is based, and the methods, evidence and information used in arriving at the conclusions, with the documents it relies on. An opinion can contain every one of those items and still not say which section it used as the reference and why: the list is satisfied, and the tribunal is left without a criterion.

What makes an opinion usable

  • 01The question, in writing. How many ploughing hours are attributable to the boulders between KP 18 and KP 30, not "what is the claim worth". An opinion that answers a wider question than the one asked ends up deciding contractual matters that are not its to decide.
  • 02The data and their definition. Which records, which sections, and how the hours are counted – stops included or excluded – identically for every section compared. Without the definition, two progress rates cannot be compared.
  • 03The choice that decides the result, with the alternatives. The reference section, the reason for choosing it, and the result the other candidate sections would give. It is the table that lets the tribunal see where the two experts stand and why.
  • 04A check independent of the comparison. The recorded stop hours, or another fact that does not depend on the choice of reference. If the result falls outside the limits that fact sets, the reference is wrong.
  • 05The uncertainties with their direction. The learning effect, the ground differences the data do not resolve: for each, whether it raises or lowers the calculated loss. A range with its reasons holds up at a hearing better than a single figure.

Finally, there is a condition that is set earlier, in the specification, and not once the dispute is open: the daily report must record for every shift the start and end KP and the ploughing hours, with stops classified by cause. Without those records no measured mile is possible, either for the claimant or for the respondent.

The underlying point

In the example the claim and the response use the same method and the same records, and differ by a factor of two and a half because of a single choice: which section represents undisturbed work. An opinion that shows that choice, the alternatives and their effect gives the tribunal a criterion. One that hides it gives it only another number.

Faced with any technical opinion, one's own or the other side's, the question to ask is therefore: which choice, if made differently, would change the result – and does the opinion say so?
