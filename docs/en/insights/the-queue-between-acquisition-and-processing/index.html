The queue between acquisition and processing: how many days pass before anyone looks at the data

The vessel's daily report says 120 km acquired, on schedule. The processing report says 80 km processed, on its own schedule. Both are right. And yet on day 8 the line just acquired will wait five days before anyone opens it, and a defect that only processing can see will meanwhile have built up 600 km of data.

Neither phase is late. The delay lies between the two, in a queue that no indicator measures. This article shows how that queue grows, what it costs – not the delivery date, which is in the schedule, but the time that passes before a defect is seen – and what to ask for in the specification.

Two schedules, both met

In a geophysical survey production is measured on board, in kilometres of line per day. Processing – corrections, mosaics, interpretation, checking the recognition criterion – works on the same lines at its own pace and to its own schedule. The contractor plans it knowing that it will finish after demobilisation, and sets the delivery of the processed data accordingly. So far nothing is wrong.

That the pace of a chain of work is set by its slowest stage is the starting point of Goldratt's theory of constraints. What matters here is a consequence that gets less attention: a queue forms in front of the slowest stage. If processing is slower than acquisition, the difference does not just move the end of the work; it builds up every day between the two phases. And the line just acquired is not processed when it arrives: it waits for everything ahead of it to be cleared.

A worked example

The figures are synthetic – built to illustrate the mechanism, not taken from real projects.

Survey of a cable route with side scan sonar, multibeam and sub-bottom profiler: 1,800 km of lines, acquired at 120 km a day over 15 days. Processing works on the previous day's lines, in order of arrival, and processes 80 km a day. For simplicity the example has no weather days and no breakdowns: both phases run at the planned pace.

The wait is the queue divided by the processing pace: the line just acquired is opened once all the kilometres ahead of it have been processed. It grows by half a day for every day of acquisition, because every day 40 km join the queue that processing does not clear: 120 in, 80 out. The last line of the survey waits eight and a half days.

Full processing ends on day 23.5: 1,800 km at 80 a day, started one day late. That is the date the contractor put in the schedule, and it is met.

What the queue costs

On day 8 the towfish is recovered for a repair and redeployed. From then on the side scan records electrical noise that does not stand out on board: the image is there across the whole range. But beyond a certain distance from the line a boulder of the required size can no longer be told apart from the noise, and the lines do not meet the recognition criterion. Processing establishes this when it checks the criterion against the data. The lines have to be rerun.

With the queue in the example, the first defective line is opened on day 13. In the five days in between the vessel has acquired 600 km with the same defect: five vessel days to rerun, on a fifteen-day survey. Had processing kept pace, with a one-day wait, the defect would have surfaced on day 9, after 120 km: one vessel day.

600 kmacquired with the defect before anyone sees it, with acquisition and processing both on their own schedule

In the example the vessel is still on site, and the remedy costs days of acquisition. But processing opens the line acquired on day 9.3 only on day 15, the last day of acquisition: any defect that starts later surfaces after demobilisation. That covers the last 680 km, more than a third of the survey.

The relationship is simple: the kilometres exposed to a defect are the daily production times the wait. Production is meant to be high; the wait is the only one of the two factors that can be reduced without slowing the vessel down. In queueing theory the relationship between queue, flow and wait is Little's law, which however holds in a steady state. With processing slower than acquisition there is no steady state: the queue grows until the vessel stops acquiring.

The quick-look in B is a reduced pass: only the checks that find the most expensive defects, the recognition criterion among them. In the example it takes a quarter of the time of full processing, and keeping pace with 120 km a day uses 37.5% of the capacity: 120 / (80 × 4). Full processing carries on with the rest, 50 km a day, and after the last line goes back to 80. Total work grows by a quarter, and delivery slips by 5.6 days.

C costs extra capacity: one more person or one more shift. B costs days ashore: it moves delivery by 5.6 days so as not to risk four vessel days. If the defect never comes, B has only lengthened the delivery. It is insurance, and it is judged as such: its cost is certain, the damage it avoids is not.

The objections

The first: onboard QC sees defects. It sees many – coverage, towfish altitude, image quality line by line. It does not see what emerges only from the processing checks: the recognition criterion applied to the data, the comparison between adjacent lines, the final corrections. The example concerns this class of defects; for the others the queue only costs time ashore.

The second: weather lets processing catch up. A standby day gives processing a day to clear 80 km of queue, and the wait drops by one day. It helps, but the queue shrinks when the sea stops the work, not when anyone decides; and in the weeks of good weather it keeps growing by 40 km a day.

The third: delivery is on time. True, and so is the contract. But the delivery date says when processing finishes, not when it starts looking at a piece of data. The two measures amount to the same thing only if both phases run at the same pace.

The fourth: the defect can be corrected in processing. Some can, and then the queue only costs time ashore. The noise in the example cannot: a boulder the sonar did not tell apart from the noise cannot be reconstructed afterwards. For this class of defects the cost is paid in vessel time, and it is proportional to the wait.

What to ask for in the specification

  • 01The wait in the daily report, next to production. Every day, the acquisition date of the oldest line not yet through processing, or through the quick-look. It is a single number, and it says for how many days there have been acquired data that nobody has looked at.
  • 02A threshold on the wait, not on the method. Two days, for instance, which at the pace of the example means 240 km exposed. How to meet it – more capacity, a quick-look, a different order of work – is for the contractor to decide; processing at 80 km a day exceeds it as early as day 2.
  • 03The content of the quick-look, in writing. Which checks are made straight away: at least those whose defects cannot be corrected in processing, starting with the recognition criterion.
  • 04The delivery date stated accordingly. If the quick-look lengthens full processing, that is known at tender rather than discovered later.

The underlying point

A production indicator says how many kilometres have been acquired; a processing indicator says how many have been processed. Neither says for how many days a piece of data has been waiting to be looked at, and that is where, between two phases both on schedule, the risk builds up. Measuring it costs one line in the daily report. Not measuring it, in the example, costs four vessel days.

References

  • J. D. C. Little, “A Proof for the Queuing Formula: L = λW”, Operations Research, 9(3), 1961, pp. 383–387: the relationship between queue, flow and wait, proved for stationary processes.
  • E. M. Goldratt, J. Cox, The Goal, North River Press, 1984: the slowest stage as the constraint on the whole chain of work.
