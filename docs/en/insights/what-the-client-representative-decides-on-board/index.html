What a client representative decides on board within the day: the segment to rerun before leaving the area

At 14:10 the positioning system loses its real-time corrections, and regains them at 14:28. The contractor reports that the segment will be recovered in post-processing and proposes to carry on. The client representative on board faces a decision nobody ashore will take for them, because by the time it is discussed ashore the vessel will be elsewhere.

The duties of a representative on board are usually listed: countersigning the daily report, witnessing calibrations, following data QC. Less is said about what they can decide, and what a deferred decision costs. This article takes a single case and follows it through: a segment of line out of specification, and the choice between rerunning it at once and waiting for post-processing to recover it.

Why the decision has a deadline

The cost of rerunning a segment of line is not fixed. It is the time to acquire it plus the time to get back to it, and the second term grows as the vessel moves away. At the end of the line the segment is a few kilometres off; a day later the vessel is working another block of the corridor; after demobilisation, rerunning it takes a vessel sent out for the purpose, and usually it is not rerun.

The post-processing result, on the other hand, comes late by construction. PPP post-processing relies on precise satellite orbits and clocks: the rapid products of the International GNSS Service (IGS) are available about 17 hours after the end of the UTC day, the final ones about 13 days after the end of the week. Faster products exist, commercial ones included, and methods that do not depend on them, but the result still comes hours after the end of the line, not before. Whoever waits to learn whether the recovery worked decides when rerunning the segment already costs more.

A worked example

The figures below are illustrative – built to show the mechanism, not taken from real projects – but the situation recurs in every campaign working with real-time corrections.

A cable corridor survey with hull-mounted multibeam, acquired at 4 knots, that is 7.408 km/h. The specification requires a horizontal positioning uncertainty no greater than 1.0 m at 95%. From 14:10 to 14:28 the system loses its corrections and the uncertainty it estimates rises to 2.5 m: eighteen minutes of line, 18 / 60 × 7.408 = 2.2 km of data out of specification.

To rerun it, the segment is acquired with 0.5 km of overlap at each end, 3.2 km in all, which at 4 knots takes 0.43 hours. To that are added a line turn with run-in, 0.25 hours, and the transit there and back at 10 knots, that is 18.52 km/h. At the end of the line the vessel is 6 km from the segment. Thirty hours later, when in the example the contractor delivers the reprocessed segment, the vessel is working a block 35 km away.

Waiting does not always cost 4.5 hours: it costs them only if the recovery fails. If it succeeds, the segment is good and nothing is rerun. The right comparison is therefore between 1.33 certain hours and 4.46 hours multiplied by the probability that the recovery fails. The two are equal when that probability is 1.33 / 4.46, about 30%. Above 30% rerunning at once pays, below it waiting does.

What can be checked in minutes

Nobody on board can estimate a recovery probability that precisely, and there is no need to. The probability depends largely on a fact that can be checked at once: whether the receiver logged raw observations for the whole interval, with a margin before and after. Post-processing works on those. If they are missing, or have gaps in exactly those eighteen minutes, there is nothing to reprocess and the probability of failure is one: the segment is rerun now.

If the observations are there and continuous, the probability drops sharply, and waiting becomes reasonable – on one condition: that the result arrives while the vessel is still close. That is where a decision taken within the day becomes a deadline, and not an act of trust.

The check takes the contractor a few minutes: the list of the receiver's raw files with their start and end times. It is a question the representative can ask before the vessel leaves the line, and the answer settles the matter better than any estimate.

The objections that will come

The first: post-processing always recovers these segments. Often true, and the example does not deny it: with raw observations logged, waiting is the cheaper choice. But the objection answers another question. The question on board is not whether recovery usually works, but whether, for this interval, the data that make it possible exist – and that can be answered before deciding.

The second: with faster products the result comes in a few hours. In that case the vessel is closer, the cost of waiting falls, and waiting pays more often. All the more reason to write the deadline into the specification: what sets the cost of waiting is not the processing method, but when the result arrives relative to where the vessel is.

The third: the representative has no authority to order additional work, which is a variation and is decided ashore. Correct, and it is why the decision has to be prepared in the contract. If the specification states that out-of-criterion data are rerun before leaving the area, the representative orders nothing: they apply a rule already signed. Who pays for the reline hours is a matter of contract, as for any other category of vessel time – the article on classifying vessel time deals with exactly that. What matters here is something else: if the recovery fails, the 3.1 hours of difference are lost, whoever bears them.

What stays ashore

The example shows the line that holds for the rest of a representative's work too. On board, what is decided is what can be compared with a written criterion and checked on the spot: a figure against a threshold, the presence of the files a promise depends on, a deadline against the vessel's position. Ashore, what is decided is what changes scope, cost or schedule beyond what the contract foresaw. A specification that does not draw this line leaves the representative choosing between two mistakes: deciding what is not theirs to decide, or deferring ashore what reaches the shore too late.

What to require in the specification

  • 01An acceptance criterion measurable on board. A threshold on the positioning uncertainty that the system records continuously, not a judgement to be formed at the end of the campaign. If the criterion can only be checked in the office, there is nothing to decide on board.
  • 02The rule for out-of-criterion data. It is rerun before leaving the area, unless the contractor demonstrates recovery by a set deadline. With the rule written, the reline is not a variation but the application of the contract.
  • 03A deadline tied to the vessel's position. The post-processing result is delivered before the vessel moves beyond a distance, or a number of hours, stated in the specification. A deadline set at final delivery is the same as none.
  • 04Continuous logging of raw observations. Throughout acquisition, with the list of files and their times available to the representative on request. It is the condition every promise of recovery depends on.
  • 05The line between what is decided on board and what goes ashore. Written down, with the name of who answers ashore and the time within which they answer. A decision deferred without a response time becomes a decision taken by the vessel's schedule.

The underlying point

The value of a representative on board lies not in the signatures but in the decisions that can only be taken while the vessel is still there. In the example the same decision costs 1.3 hours at the end of the line and 4.5 thirty hours later, and the fact that settles it – raw observations logged or not – is available in minutes.

For every promise of recovery made on board, the question to ask is therefore this: which data does it depend on, do they already exist, and when will we know whether it worked, relative to where the vessel will be?
