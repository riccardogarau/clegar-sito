Where parallel paths meet: three paths at 80% or better, one milestone at 56%

The geotechnical vessel is chartered from day 70. To start work it needs three things: the geophysical survey charts with the CPT locations, UXO clearance of the same locations, and the permit from the maritime authority. The schedule shows all three ready in time, each with at least ten days of margin, and the risk analysis of each path gives a probability between 80 and 86% of finishing by day 70. The probability that all three are ready is 56%.

None of the three paths contains an error. The defect lies at the point where they meet, and it does not appear in a schedule read one path at a time. This article shows where that 56% comes from, why margin in days does not reveal it, what it takes to bring it back above 80%, and what to ask for so that the number appears before the charter is signed and not on the day the vessel goes on standby.

A worked example

The figures are synthetic – built to illustrate the method, not taken from real projects.

An offshore wind farm at site investigation stage. Three parallel paths, awarded to different parties, feed a single milestone: the start of the geotechnical campaign, with the vessel chartered from day 70 after kick-off. For each path the duration is estimated as a minimum, a most likely value and a maximum; for the two surveys the range accounts for weather, for the permit for the authority's review time.

Durations in days from kick-off. The probabilities come from a triangular distribution built on the three values, rounded to the nearest percentage point; the calculations that follow start from the rounded values.

The calculation belongs where the paths meet

The vessel can start only if all three paths are complete. If their delays are independent, the probability that none of them is late is the product of the three probabilities:

0.82 × 0.86 × 0.80 = 0.56

56%the probability that on day 70 the geotechnical vessel finds all three things it needs to start work ready

Taken on its own, no path is at risk: the least certain finishes on time four times out of five. But one late path is enough to make the milestone late, and there are three paths that can be late. The GAO Schedule Assessment Guide calls this effect merge bias: at the point where parallel paths meet, risk multiplies, the probability of being on time falls with every path that converges there, and it is one of the reasons why even a well-built schedule tends to finish later than the date it shows.

Why the margin does not show it

The integrated schedule shows path A as the critical path, with ten days of margin before the vessel, and path C as the most comfortable, with fifteen days of float. Read that way, the permit is the path to worry about least. It is in fact the one with the lowest probability.

Margin measures the distance between the most likely value and the date. It does not say how far the duration stretches when things go wrong. A permit review usually closes in 55 days, but a request for further information or an opinion from another body can take it to 86: thirty-one days beyond the most likely value, against twenty for the geophysical survey. Fifteen days of margin cover less than half of those thirty-one; the ten days of the geophysical survey cover exactly half of its twenty. That is why the path with the most float is the one that arrives on time least often.

The objections

The first: the paths are not independent. The two surveys work in the same sea and the same season, and a week of heavy weather stops both. That is true, and the dependence works in the favourable direction: if the delays of A and B tend to coincide, the probability that both arrive on time is higher than the product. The limiting case is that they coincide entirely: then A and B are ready together with the probability of the less certain of the two, 82%, and the calculation becomes 0.82 × 0.80 = 0.66. The permit does not depend on weather and remains independent regardless. The true probability therefore lies between 56 and 66%: in any case far from the 80% that each path, on its own, suggests.

The second: the path probabilities are estimates, and different estimates give a different number. That is also true, and it is a reason to make them, not to leave them out. A deterministic schedule does not avoid the estimate: it makes it without declaring it, showing a date without saying with what probability it will be met. Changing the estimates by a few points changes the result by a few points; it does not change the fact that three probabilities around 80%, multiplied together, give little more than half.

The third: the vessel can start on the locations that are already ready. That is not an objection but the first of the solutions, and it is worth looking at with numbers.

Move the date, or change what the vessel waits for

The obvious solution is to start the vessel later. Redoing the product with the probabilities of the three paths at each date:

To get above 80% the vessel has to start on day 75: five days of delay on everything that follows the geotechnical campaign, decided before anyone knows whether they were needed.

The alternative is to ask what the vessel actually needs on day 70. It does not need the complete geophysical survey: it needs the charts for the first locations it will investigate. If processing delivers the block containing them first, path A finishes six days earlier for that part, and its probability of being ready by day 70 rises from 82 to 97%. In the same way, the permit application can be filed ten days earlier, for an area that contains every possible location rather than for the final list: the probability of path C rises from 80 to 97%.

ActionABCMilestone at day 70
None82%86%80%56%

Both97%86%97%81%

Neither action on its own takes the milestone above 70%: improving one path leaves the weaker of the other two as it was, and in the product it weighs as much as before. Together they bring it to 81% without moving the vessel. The first acts on the critical path; the second on the path with the most float, which a schedule read by margins would not have pointed to.

The rest of the geophysical survey does not stop being a predecessor: it becomes one for locations the vessel will reach weeks later. The merge point does not disappear; it moves to where there is time to absorb a delay. This is also what the GAO recommends: examining activities with many predecessors, to check whether all of them are needed and whether different logic can link some of them to other activities.

What to ask for in planning

  • 01A schedule risk analysis, not just a schedule. For every path feeding a milestone with contractual consequences – a charter, a weather window, a delivery – the minimum, most likely and maximum duration, and what each one depends on.
  • 02The probability stated for the milestone, not for the paths. The question is with what probability the vessel finds everything ready on the day the charter starts, not whether each path has margin. With the method stated: the product for a few independent paths, a Monte Carlo simulation of the schedule when the paths are many or linked.
  • 03Dependencies between paths declared. Which paths share a cause of delay – weather, the same vessel, the same processing team – and how they were treated. Treating as independent two paths that are late together lowers the calculated probability; treating them as linked when they are not raises it.
  • 04The milestone's predecessors checked one by one. For each: is all of it needed, or is part of it enough? A milestone tied to a whole deliverable, when one block of it is enough, adds a path to the merge point for no reason.
  • 05The calculation redone when a path changes. A delay on a path that had margin moves no date in the schedule, but it lowers the probability at the merge point. That is where it has to be read.

The underlying point

A schedule read one path at a time answers, for each path, the question "is it on time?". At the point where the paths meet, the question changes: not whether each one is on time, but whether all of them are together. Three reassuring answers to the first question can give, to the second, little better than a coin toss.
